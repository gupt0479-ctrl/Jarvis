Guatemala.  
  
The 1950s were, in my opinion, Guatemala's most volatile decade following World War II. After a coup supported by foreign powers, the nation went from a promising phase of democratic reform to political anarchy throughout these years. A protracted and bloody civil war finally resulted from the changes that started with land reform and ended with military authority.

Jacobo Árbenz was one of the most significant figures during this time. During his presidency, Árbenz sought to modernise Guatemala by giving impoverished farmers access to underused land. Many rural families supported his ideas, but the United Fruit Company and affluent landowners were put in danger. During the Cold War, his reforms also sparked American concerns that Guatemala was headed toward communism.

Carlos Castillo Armas was another important person. He spearheaded the 1954 coup that ousted Árbenz from government with backing from the U.S. Central Intelligence Agency. Castillo Armas strengthened political repression and undid many of the reforms after seizing office. His actions put an end to Guatemala's democratic experiment and contributed to the instability that followed for many years.

Cuba.  
  
I would also pick the 1950s as the most turbulent decade for Cuba. Growing popular discontent, armed uprisings, and ultimately a revolution that fundamentally altered the nation's political and economic structure all occurred during this period.

Fulgencio Batista, who took power in a military coup in 1952 and ruled Cuba as a dictator, was the first significant figure. His administration was infamous for its tight ties to powerful corporate interests, corruption, and censorship. Growing opposition was fueled by the perception among many Cubans that his administration disregarded the demands of common people.

Fidel Castro, who spearheaded a revolutionary movement against Batista's regime, was the second important person. Castro successfully started a guerilla war from the Sierra Maestra mountains after rebuilding his forces despite the failure of his initial attempts. Castro seized power in Cuba after Batista left the nation in 1959. His triumph signaled the start of a communist regime that fundamentally altered Cuban society and its ties to the US.

The political tensions of the Cold War had a significant impact on both Cuba and Guatemala, despite the fact that they underwent turmoil for different causes. Decades of violence followed the coup that put a stop to reform attempts in Guatemala. Widespread discontent with the regime in Cuba led to a successful revolution that changed the country. These significant turning points were largely shaped by the choices and leadership of Fulgencio Batista and Fidel Castro in Cuba, as well as Jacobo Árbenz and Carlos Castillo Armas in Guatemala.

Question to the classmates: Do you believe that foreign intervention exacerbated the conflict?
### My reply to the response 
Yes, straight up - Guatemala shows what really happened. A coup backed by the CIA in 1954 did not bring peace; instead, conflict smoldered until full war erupted in 1960, lasting 36 years. Around 200,000 died before it ended in 1996, most of them Indigenous Maya people caught in violence. According to a truth commission funded by the United Nations and published in 1999, government forces carried out 93 percent of those killings during U.S.-supported anti-rebel operations. Efforts aimed at blocking leftist rule wound up unleashing generations of official brutality. As for Cuba, things spiraled differently after April 1961, when an assault at the Bay of Pigs collapsed completely. Fidel Castro turned defeat into triumph through public messaging, then made socialism official by May - aligning more tightly with Moscow than he may initially have intended.

Nicaragua - The 1980s In Turmoil

Opening with upheaval, Nicaragua’s 1980s became its most turbulent postwar era. By the time the years ran out, conflict had worn down both state and economy.
One morning in July 1979, the Sandinista National Liberation Front removed Anastasio Somoza Debayle from power, closing a chapter of four decades shaped by his family's control. After escaping, Somoza settled in Paraguay; there, violence caught up with him when he was killed the following year. Washington viewed the new leadership in Managua with deep unease, seeing it as a threat due to its leftist direction. Instead of diplomacy, officials chose covert backing for rebel forces known as the Contras. According to Jones, these fighters targeted civilians linked to reform - teachers, health workers, and activists - which stirred debate across the United States about involvement abroad. By 1984, pressure grew so strong that lawmakers cut off funds and arms shipments meant for the opposition groups. Even so, the Reagan government kept backing the Contras secretly - funneling money via unauthorized weapon deals with Iran, which exposed the Iran-Contra affair in 1986. A full ten years of conflict had passed by the time Daniel Ortega’s Sandinista movement was defeated at the polls by Violeta Chamorro in 1990.
It was Somoza Debayle who turned resistance into something unavoidable. Ruling began for his family back in 1936, a long grip on power. After the 1972 quake hit Managua, aid meant for survivors vanished - trust faded fast among city professionals. When soldiers opened fire in 1978, anger spread; then came Chamorro’s killing, which pulled separate dissenters together. FSLN stayed small until cruelty gave it purpose and people.
Once leading rebels, Daniel Ortega reshaped the FSLN into a political force. Following the 1979 revolution, he took charge of the ruling junta before winning presidency in 1984 by securing two-thirds support. Under his leadership, fighting continued with U.S.-backed Contra forces. Wide-scale reading programs began alongside medical access and redistribution of farmland - yet draft policies emerged along with media controls. Power passed without violence when Violeta Chamorro won in 1990, marking official closure to years of conflict.

Haiti in the 1990s - A Decade of Unrest

Haiti’s postwar years saw greatest upheaval in the 1990s - within four short years, a freely chosen leader took office, only to be toppled by force; foreign troops arrived soon after, then departed once civilian rule returned.
Come winter 1990, Jean-Bertrand Aristide took office after Haiti’s inaugural truly open presidential vote. By mid-1991, power shifted when General Raoul Cédras seized control through armed takeover, sending Aristide overseas. Violence spread fast under the new military rule - countless lives lost, entire communities scattered. Meanwhile, global sanctions tightened, grinding daily commerce to a halt across the nation. Back in 1994, U.S. forces stepped in under President Clinton, putting Aristide back in office. Once he completed his time in charge, power moved without conflict to René Préval by 1996.
A Catholic priest turned politician, Jean-Bertrand Aristide relied on widespread public backing rather than an organized party network. Because his overwhelming victory in 1990 unsettled long-standing military and business powers, a swift overthrow followed. When American forces helped return him to power in 1994, it was tied to demands - IMF reform policies plus negotiated concessions. His second win, in 2000, unfolded amid controversy, reigniting unrest that rippled through the years ahead.
Backed by the army, Raoul Cédras took power through force. Leading Haiti’s military, he led the overthrow in September 1991, ruling alongside FRAPH - a militia group acting as his main enforcer. Under him, executions, abductions, and armed chaos turned into regular methods of control. Only when U.S. forces arrived did pressure build enough to push him out. He stepped down in 1994 once resistance seemed futile.

Nicaragua's democratic progress was reversed by US-funded external pressure while Haiti's was reversed by an internal military coup - which do you think is harder for a country to recover from?
