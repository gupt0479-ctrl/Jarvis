Liberia's path into the contemporary world has been shaped by two deeply interconnected problems: the entrenched exclusion of indigenous peoples from political power, and the devastating civil wars that exclusion eventually produced.

From independence in 1847, Liberia was governed by a small Americo-Liberian Creole elite — descendants of freed American slaves — who controlled political and economic life while the indigenous majority, roughly 95% of the population, was largely shut out. The right to vote wasn't even extended to indigenous Liberians until 1946. This structural exclusion festered for over a century. Growing corruption and economic mismanagement under President Tolbert — who filled his cabinet with family members and failed to address rising food prices — finally triggered a breaking point. In April 1980, Master Sergeant Samuel Doe led a military coup, killing Tolbert and publicly executing his entire cabinet. It was the first military overthrow in Liberian history. Doe replaced one form of exclusion with another: rather than reform the system, he packed his government with members of his own Krahn ethnic group, deepening tribal resentments that had been building for generations.

Those resentments exploded into civil war in December 1989 when Charles Taylor's National Patriotic Front of Liberia (NPLF) invaded from the Ivory Coast. Taylor's forces, backed by Libya's Qaddafi, drew from Americo-Liberians and northern tribal groups — particularly the Mano and Gio — who had suffered brutal reprisals under Doe. Years of fighting followed. All three factions in the subsequent conflict used child soldiers, some as young as ten. Even after Taylor won elections in 1996, peace didn't hold; full-scale fighting resumed by 2000. The textbook describes how Taylor "basically gave militia groups loyal to him a free hand to terrorize and rob the population as a form of compensation, which alienated him from most Liberians, who accused Taylor of ruling the country like a warlord" (Jones, p. 223). UN sanctions over blood diamonds and a war crimes indictment finally forced him into exile in 2003, and democratic elections in 2005 brought Ellen Johnson Sirleaf to power — the first woman elected head of state in African history.

Turning to Sudan and South Sudan, two issues have driven persistent instability. The first is religious and ethnic civil war: the Muslim-dominated north imposed Arabization and Islamization on the mostly Christian south after independence in 1956, sparking conflicts that stretched across two wars (1955–72 and 1983–2003) and killed over two million people. The second issue is oil. When oil was discovered in southern Sudan in 1979, the northern government immediately violated the 1972 peace treaty by redrawing regional boundaries to consolidate control over those revenues — directly triggering the Second Civil War. Even after South Sudan's independence in 2011, border disputes over shared oil pipelines continued to fuel tension between the two countries.

Comparing Liberia with Sudan and South Sudan, the clearest similarity is that both were shaped by the political exclusion of ethnic or regional majorities — exclusion that made civil war almost inevitable. In both cases, postcolonial power structures concentrated authority in a minority while leaving the majority without representation. The key difference is outcome. Liberia, despite two brutal civil wars, remained unified and achieved a genuine democratic transition by 2005. Sudan fractured permanently — South Sudan split off in 2011 and then fell into its own civil war shortly after. Sudan's conflict also carried a hard ideological dimension (Islam vs. Christianity, then Arab vs. non-Arab in Darfur) that made compromise far more difficult than in Liberia, where the conflict was primarily about political access rather than religion.

**Question for classmates:** Given that both Liberia and Sudan experienced civil wars rooted in the long-term exclusion of ethnic majorities from power, why do you think Liberia was able to reach a stable democratic transition in 2005 while Sudan still hasn't managed one decades later?
