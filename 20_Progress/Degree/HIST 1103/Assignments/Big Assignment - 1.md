Part A: Three countries change over time since 1950

China (East Asia)

Founded in 1949, Mao’s People Republic entered the new decade still fragile - its economy shattered by conflict, its countryside struggling without aid from the West. Assistance came at first from Moscow but faded fast when political differences grew sharp around 1960. Instead of private plots, land was merged into collective units while villagers shifted work to backyard furnaces during the campaign known as the Great Leap Forward. Then drought hit. With crops failing and pressure rising, starvation spread widely between 1958 and 1962 - the toll reaching tens of millions according to later estimates (Jones 2025; Spence 2013, 553). Starting in 1966, Mao began the Cultural Revolution out of personal shame and fading authority, aiming to regain power by removing opponents. Red Guards dismantled universities, targeted scholars and death estimates reach about two million (Spence 2013, 575). With Mao gone by 1976, Deng Xiaoping slowly rose until holding full command around 1978 - then changed direction entirely. Household farming replaced collective agriculture while overseas investment gained access to shore regions. Nearly a decade of almost 10% yearly economic expansion lifted around 800 million people above poverty levels (Jones 2025). Entry into the World Trade Organization happened in 2001, whereas Olympic games took place eight years later. Since 2012 under Xi Jinping, pressure increased across maritime claims, Hong Kong affairs, and international construction projects via the Belt and Road effort.

Iran (Middle East)

Back then, Iran’s throne existed in name only - actual authority rested within its elected leaders and Mossadegh’s office. Nationalizing oil assets during 1951 triggered covert moves by British and American agents two years later; their hidden operation restored the monarch as sole ruler (Keddie 2006, 134). Brutality became routine under SAVAK, silencing opposition through pain. Meanwhile, sweeping reforms pushed from above angered religious figures along with radicals on the far left. Discontent had already spread across social classes by 1978; in early 1979, Khomeini came back from exile as Iran transformed into an Islamic Republic (Jones 2025; Keddie 2006, 230). With the takeover of the U.S. Embassy that November, relations with America ended for good. Then, in September 1980, Iraq attacked - launching a conflict lasting eight years and claiming nearly one million lives. Once Khomeini passed away in 1989, leadership shifted repeatedly between reformers and hardliners, yet failed to bridge the divide between religious authority and city-dwelling, educated Iranians. In 2022, nationwide unrest erupted after Mahsa Amini died while detained by the morality police - the biggest threat to the government since it first gained power (Jones 2025).

Vietnam (Southeast Asia)

Midway through the 1950s, Vietnam saw heavy conflict with French forces. Victory at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954 came under Ho Chi Minh’s leadership, ending colonial presence. Following that battle, national division emerged along the 17th parallel - one half aligned with communism, the other supported by Washington (Karnow 1997, 197). Troop numbers climbed; eventually more than half a million U.S. soldiers arrived. Stability never took root. After the Paris Peace Accords halted American participation in 1973, Saigon collapsed two years later, by April of 1975. Once unified, Vietnam introduced state-led economic models while opening re-education centers, then moved into Cambodia by 1978 - an act that triggered China's military response in 1979. Economic output declined sharply during this stretch. Change began under Doi Moi reforms starting in 1986: private businesses returned, overseas capital flowed in, yet the ruling party retained authority. From there, yearly growth exceeded seven percent throughout the next decade. Relations with Washington resumed in 1995, afterward entry into the World Trade Organization arrived by 2007 (Jones 2025; Karnow 1997, 44).

Part B: Key People and Events

China

Besides guiding China between 1949 and 1976, Mao Zedong sent troops into Korea in 1950 to strengthen national boundaries. In 1972, unexpectedly, he welcomed President Nixon, shifting foreign relations. Yet under his direction occurred the worst demographic collapses ever seen in the country’s past. Official assessments claim seven-tenths of his choices were sound, three-tenths flawed - a judgment that softens reality far too much (Spence 2013, 544). Years passed without higher education during the Cultural Revolution, which ran from 1966 to 1976. As many as two million people died under its course. Deng Xiaoping disappeared from power on two separate occasions, later working in a tractor plant (Spence 2013, 572). The trauma left behind shaped future leaders’ deep unease toward disorder in thought, a mindset that continues to support strict oversight today. 

Starting in 1978, Deng’s changes took apart the collective farms, created special economic zones, also brought in profit-based rewards while keeping tight control within the ruling party. A phrase often quoted - “whether a cat is black or white matters less than its ability to catch mice” - summed up the turn from strict doctrine to measurable outcomes (Jones 2025). Yet gaps between rich and poor widened sharply; environmental harm increased alongside social tensions, including the events at Tiananmen that revealed clear limits on any move toward open governance.

Iran

Mossadegh served as Iran’s democratically chosen leader between 1951 and 1953, standing as strong proof that democracy could take root in the country. Yet his time in power came undone when foreign forces stepped in through Operation Ajax. Because of that moment, deep anger settled into public life - silent at first, then shaping events years later. That bitterness helped fuel transformations across the nation’s politics, reaching even into the upheaval of 1979 (Keddie 2006, 135). Under Khomeini, religious scholars gained official control through velayat-e faqih - a shift unseen before in Islamic governance structures. Diplomatic ties between Iran and the United States collapsed overnight. Neighboring royal families grew uneasy watching revolutionary fervor spread nearby. Conflict erupted soon after, dragging into a prolonged war lasting nearly a decade (Jones 2025; Keddie 2006, 238). 

Beginning in 1980, the conflict between Iran and Iraq unfolded after Saddam concluded that revolutionary chaos had left Tehran vulnerable. After nearly a decade of brutal combat, poison gas assaults, and global disapproval, Iran remained standing. Surviving such pressure strengthened the government’s belief it could endure any threat - this confidence eventually helped trigger moves toward Kuwait by 1990 (Keddie 2006, 252).

Vietnam

Outliving two dominant Western forces, Ho Chi Minh anchored his cause in village-level loyalty instead of battlefield power. Death came in 1969 - well before the nation joined again in 1975. A metropolis now bearing his name shows clearly how Vietnam recalls its past leader (Karnow 1997, 148). Though the Tet Offensive failed militarily - nearly 45,000 Viet Cong lost within weeks - it shifted perception. More than 100 urban centers attacked despite truce expectations. Walter Cronkite labeled the war unwinnable. Afterward, President Johnson declined to seek re-election (Karnow 1997, 537). 

After Doi Moi began in 1986, state-controlled farms faded as private businesses emerged alongside incoming investment - yet political control remained unchanged. By the next decade, rice production rose so rapidly that Vietnam became one of the world’s leading exporters. During the 1990s, economic expansion never dipped below 7% each year, since living conditions improved and fewer people lived in poverty (Jones 2025).

Part C: Deep Dive Analysis

Deng Xiaoping’s Changes and How They Shaped China

China stood still through much of the 1970s - isolated from global investment, lagging in technology, and slowly recovering from the damage done during the Cultural Revolution to its skilled workers. The countryside shifted early; collective farm targets gave way to family-based agreements, allowing extra harvest to be sold freely. Production rose fast - proof, really, that rewards could drive results more effectively than top-down orders (Spence 2013, 617). Following this shift came zones like Shenzhen: launched in 1980 with just thirty thousand residents, it swelled past ten million by the early 2000s (Jones 2025). Following its 2001 WTO accession, China became tied more tightly into world commerce - a linkage that fed its own expansion.

Starting strong in the early 1990s, growth hovered close to 10 percent each year across three decades. Around eight hundred million people rose beyond conditions of severe deprivation during that stretch. Resource needs swelled, pulling African and Latin American exporters into sharper focus. Lower-priced goods flowed into high-income nations, restraining inflationary pressure on household essentials. At the same time, factory jobs vanished in those regions, undercut by competitive pricing overseas. This imbalance helped fuel discontent, later shaping political currents throughout the following decade. 

On June 4, 1989, events took a sharp turn. Though growth had lifted living standards, demands grew anyway. Protests swelled in Beijing that spring - students first, then city laborers joining later - as voices called for change. Casualties reached into the hundreds, perhaps beyond. Still today, China remains positioned exactly there - where that boundary was drawn. The tension seen now with democratic states traces back mostly to this divide: economic change accepted, political shift refused (Spence 2013, 630).

The 1979 Iranian Islamic Revolution and Its Effects

Out of thin air, the uprising did not emerge. Foreign interference in national affairs became clear after events of 1953. Over time, state surveillance under SAVAK deepened public resentment. Come 1977, dissent spanned a wide spectrum - religious leaders, radicals, business owners, patriots - not united by ideology but by rejection (Keddie 2006, 225). Survival defined the moment, not borders drawn after fighting stopped. Power shifted when Khomeini’s idea of religious rule offered direction, not only opposition. By late 1979, taking the American embassy hardened anger between nations - a rift still open (Jones 2025). Then Baghdad struck in 1980. Nearly ten years burned through lives - close to one million gone. Yet staying standing became its own victory story.

Years passed, still echoes of the uprising moved through distant actors. From 1982, Hezbollah in Lebanon carried Iranian aims along with local ambitions. Elsewhere - in Yemen, Iraq, Gaza - parallel ties took shape, extending power beyond what armies alone might achieve. Since 1979, Gulf rulers have watched closely, funding opposition forces just to hold ground. That balance of push and resistance shaped regional strife across forty years (Keddie 2006, 253; Jones 2025). Within Iran itself, dissent grew quiet but never vanished: signs appeared in 2009 with the Green Movement, then again in 2022 when unrest returned. A revolution-born government today silences uprisings it once led. Without deep shifts, the divide only widens.

The Tet Offensive and Its Impact

Well into 1967, President Johnson's government kept insisting things were moving forward in Vietnam - even as half a million American soldiers arrived and annual costs climbed to thirty billion dollars, backed by reports saying victory might still be possible (Karnow 1997, 513). Then, overnight between January 30 and 31, 1968, around eighty thousand forces from the North and southern insurgents struck more than a hundred urban centers in violation of the Tet truce. In Hue, control shifted entirely; reclaiming it required nearly four weeks of close-quarters fighting. Though communist troops suffered heavy losses - nearly 45,000 killed early on - their weakened strength didn’t dominate U.S. headlines. Instead, what filled living rooms was Eddie Adams’ image: a prisoner falling mid-step in a Saigon alley. Walter Cronkite’s words carried even further weight; his conclusion - that victory seemed out of reach - struck deeper than official statements ever could. When Johnson said losing Cronkite meant losing average Americans, he wasn’t merely venting. He admitted something larger: trust in Washington had already unraveled beyond repair.

Fast came the consequences. In New Hampshire, McCarthy pushed close to defeating Johnson. On March 31, Johnson stepped away. The plea from Westmoreland - for 206,000 additional soldiers, was turned down. Nixon took office in autumn after pledging loosely to conclude the conflict; withdrawal of American troops started under Vietnamization, leading eventually to the 1973 Paris agreement (Jones 2025). Beyond combat outcomes, Tet revealed something else: confidence from authorities tends to fade when images on television tell another story - this idea echoed later during Watergate, influenced how journalists were handled in the Gulf War, then reappeared ahead of discussions about invading Iraq in 2003 (Karnow 1997, 19).
