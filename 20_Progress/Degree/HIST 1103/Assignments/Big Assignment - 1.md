**Part A: Three Countries Change Over Time Since 1950**

**China (East Asia)**

Mao's PRC entered 1950 still fragile—economy shattered, countryside impoverished, no Western support. Soviet help arrived and left by the early 1960s as ideological tensions widened. The Great Leap Forward (1958–62) collapsed into the deadliest famine in history—15 to 45 million dead (Jones 2025; Spence 2013, 553). The Cultural Revolution (1966–76) followed, closing universities for a decade and killing up to two million (Spence 2013, 575). Deng Xiaoping reversed course in 1978: market reforms, coastal Special Economic Zones, and nearly 10% GDP growth annually for three decades lifted roughly 800 million out of poverty (Jones 2025). Tiananmen 1989 permanently closed the door on political reform. Under Xi Jinping after 2012, Beijing pushed assertively on the South China Sea, Hong Kong, and global infrastructure through Belt and Road.

**Iran (Middle East)**

Mossadegh nationalized oil in 1951; the U.S. and UK ran Operation Ajax in 1953, removing him and restoring the Shah to unchecked power (Keddie 2006, 134). SAVAK's repression built pressure for two decades until Khomeini's 1979 revolution replaced the monarchy with an Islamic Republic (Jones 2025; Keddie 2006, 230). The U.S. Embassy seizure that November broke ties with Washington for good. Iraq invaded in September 1980—eight years of war, close to a million dead. Iran oscillated between reformist and hardline rule after 1989, while sanctions over its nuclear program deepened the crisis. The 2022 death of Mahsa Amini in morality police custody triggered the largest protests since the revolution's founding (Jones 2025).

**Vietnam (Southeast Asia)**

Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh defeated France at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954; the Geneva Accords divided the country at the 17th parallel (Karnow 1997, 197). U.S. involvement peaked at over 500,000 troops but couldn't stabilize the south—Saigon fell in April 1975. Post-unification Vietnam imposed socialist economics, invaded Cambodia in 1978, and drew a Chinese punitive strike in 1979. The 1986 Doi Moi reforms ended central planning, opened private enterprise, and drove 7%+ annual growth through the 1990s. Ties with the U.S. restored in 1995, WTO entry followed in 2007 (Jones 2025; Karnow 1997, 44).

**Part B: Key People and Events**

**China**

**_Mao Zedong_**: Led China from 1949 to 1976. Stabilized borders by entering Korea in 1950, tested a nuclear weapon in 1964, opened diplomacy with Nixon in 1972. He also caused the two largest mass-death events in Chinese history. The Party's official verdict—"70% correct, 30% wrong"—understates the damage considerably (Spence 2013, 544).

**_The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)_**: Shut universities for a decade, sent intellectuals and officials to labor camps or worse. Deaths reached up to two million; Deng Xiaoping was purged twice and sent to work in a tractor factory (Spence 2013, 572). Its lasting effect is a deep institutional fear of ideological disorder that still justifies tight political control today.

**_Deng Xiaoping's 1978 Reforms_**: Dismantled commune farming, built Special Economic Zones, introduced market incentives without loosening party rule. His line—"doesn't matter if a cat is black or white as long as it catches mice"—captured the shift from doctrine to results (Jones 2025). Poverty fell at a pace never seen before. Tiananmen then showed exactly how far political reform was permitted to go.

**Iran**

**_Mohammad Mossadegh_**: Iran's elected prime minister, 1951–1953, and its best argument that democratic governance was possible there. Operation Ajax ended that. His removal planted a resentment that structured Iranian politics for seventy years, running directly into 1979 (Keddie 2006, 135).

**_The 1979 Islamic Revolution_**: Proved a mass movement could topple a U.S.-backed military state. Khomeini's *velayat-e faqih* gave clerics formal governing authority—something genuinely new in the Muslim world. It severed U.S.-Iran relations, alarmed Gulf monarchies, inspired Islamist movements regionally, and triggered an eight-year war (Jones 2025; Keddie 2006, 238).

**_The Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988)_**: Saddam invaded expecting a quick win against a weakened revolutionary state. He got eight years of warfare, chemical attacks, and economic ruin with no territorial change at the end. Iran's survival hardened the regime's sense of endurance and set off the chain that reached Kuwait in 1990 (Keddie 2006, 252).

**Vietnam**

**_Ho Chi Minh_**: Built a movement capable of outlasting two Western powers by rooting it in rural political loyalty rather than conventional force. Died in September 1969, six years before reunification. The city renamed after him reflects where he sits in Vietnamese national memory (Karnow 1997, 148).

**_The Tet Offensive (January 1968)_**: Over 100 cities hit simultaneously during the New Year ceasefire. Communist forces lost around 45,000 fighters in weeks—a military failure. But images of the U.S. Embassy under fire reached American living rooms, Cronkite called the war a stalemate, and Johnson dropped out of the race (Karnow 1997, 537).

**_Doi Moi (1986)_**: Ended collective farming, opened private enterprise and foreign capital, party control intact. Rice output jumped enough to make Vietnam a top global exporter within a decade. GDP growth averaged above 7% annually through the 1990s while poverty dropped sharply (Jones 2025).

**Part C: Deep Dive Analysis**

**Deng Xiaoping's Reforms and Their Impact on China**

By 1978, China's GDP sat at roughly $149 billion with almost no foreign investment flowing in. Deng's Household Responsibility System replaced collective farm quotas with family contracts—grain output jumped from 305 million tons in 1978 to 407 million tons by 1984 (Spence 2013, 617). Special Economic Zones followed: Shenzhen went from a fishing village of 30,000 in 1980 to over 10 million people two decades later, generating more annual export revenue than all of China had in 1978 (Jones 2025). WTO entry in December 2001 locked China permanently into global supply chains. By 2019, GDP had crossed $14 trillion and approximately 800 million people had escaped extreme poverty—the largest such reduction in recorded history (Jones 2025; Spence 2013, 640). Commodity demand from China lifted exporting economies across Africa and Latin America; cheap Chinese manufacturing held down consumer prices in wealthy countries while simultaneously displacing factory workers in the U.S. and Europe, feeding political discontent through the 2010s.

June 4, 1989 defines the political ceiling on all this. Students and urban workers filled Tiananmen Square through spring demanding reform. Deng ordered the military response; hundreds died, possibly more. The Tank Man photograph became one of the most reproduced images of the 20th century and told the world exactly what Deng's model was: prosperity without political rights (Spence 2013, 630). That line has held since, and it is the source of nearly every confrontation between Beijing and democratic governments over trade, technology, Hong Kong, and Taiwan.

**The 1979 Iranian Islamic Revolution and Its Lasting Effects**

The context starts in 1953. Operation Ajax removed Mossadegh and demonstrated that Iranian sovereignty was negotiable when Western oil interests were at stake (Keddie 2006, 134). SAVAK reinforced the lesson over two decades. By 1977, the coalition against the Shah crossed class and ideological lines—clergy, leftists, merchants, nationalists—united mainly by shared opposition. Khomeini returned on February 1, 1979, to crowds in the millions. His *velayat-e faqih* placed Islamic jurists at the top of the political hierarchy, overriding elected institutions—a genuinely new governing model in the Muslim world. The U.S. Embassy was seized on November 4, 1979; 52 diplomats held for 444 days, breaking U.S.-Iran relations and helping end Jimmy Carter's presidency (Jones 2025). Saddam invaded September 22, 1980. Iraq used chemical weapons throughout the war and killed approximately 5,000 Kurdish civilians at Halabja in March 1988 alone. Iran lost between 300,000 and 500,000 military dead but came out intact—survival became the regime's founding narrative (Keddie 2006, 253).

Regionally, Iran built influence through proxies: Hezbollah in Lebanon from 1982, Houthi networks in Yemen, Shia militias in Iraq after 2003, and armed groups in Gaza—extending reach far beyond what its conventional forces could manage (Jones 2025). Internally, the 2009 Green Movement and the 2022 uprising—160 cities, 15,000+ arrested, 500+ killed—show that the government born from a popular revolution now suppresses popular movements with the same tools the Shah used.

**The Tet Offensive and Its Impact**

By late 1967, Westmoreland told Congress the end in Vietnam was coming into view. On January 30–31, 1968, roughly 80,000 communist forces hit over 100 cities during the Tet ceasefire—including the U.S. Embassy compound in Saigon. In Hue, 26 days of urban combat ended with the discovery of mass graves holding approximately 2,800 executed civilians (Karnow 1997, 545). Militarily, the offensive was a communist disaster—around 45,000 dead in the first weeks, Viet Cong main-force capacity permanently broken. None of that mattered to the political outcome. Eddie Adams' photograph of Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan shooting a bound prisoner on a Saigon street on February 1 won the Pulitzer and circulated globally. On February 27, Cronkite concluded on CBS that the war was heading toward stalemate. Johnson reportedly said losing Cronkite meant losing Middle America—an admission that his administration's credibility had collapsed entirely (Karnow 1997, 548).

McCarthy won 42% of the New Hampshire primary on March 12. Johnson dropped out March 31. Westmoreland's request for 206,000 additional troops was denied. Nixon won November 1968 on a vague promise to end the war; Vietnamization followed, withdrawing U.S. forces toward the Paris Peace Accords of January 1973 (Jones 2025). The deeper legacy was the credibility gap Tet made permanent—the baseline suspicion that official war narratives don't match reality. It shaped press access rules in the 1991 Gulf War and resurfaced directly in debates over intelligence claims before the 2003 Iraq invasion (Karnow 1997, 19).
