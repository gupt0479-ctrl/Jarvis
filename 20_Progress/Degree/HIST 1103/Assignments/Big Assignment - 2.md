East Africa Rwanda civil war genocide
Warning signs preceded Rwanda’s tragic genocide. Colonial rule under Belgium reshaped existing social groupings - Hutus mainly farming, Tutsis largely herding - into fixed ethnic classes backed by legislation. During the 1930s, authorities distributed official ID cards marking each person’s supposed ethnicity, freezing boundaries once flexible in daily life. With colonial support, Tutsi leaders gained power over the larger Hutu population, fueling long-standing bitterness. After independence came in 1962, political figures among the Hutus tapped into that anger. Between 1962 and 1990, repeated outbreaks targeting Tutsis - peaking notably in 1959, 1963, and 1973 - pushed thousands toward exile in Uganda; there, displaced individuals formed part of what became known as the Rwandese Patriotic Front (RPF).
April 6, 1994 set it off. As President Habyarimana’s aircraft neared Kigali airport, it was struck by fire; attacks erupted in the city before dawn, reaching rural areas soon after. Over Radio Milles Collines, George Rutaganda delivered a coded phrase - “cut the tall trees” - prompting Interahamwe units to move through neighborhoods, killing residents at home using machetes and guns. The numbers shocked observers: around 800,000 Tutsis and Hutu moderates died in about three months, amounting to more than one-tenth of Rwanda’s people. In fact, Habyarimana’s administration had quietly armed and trained militias for weeks ahead, readying them for such an event.[2]
A global breakdown made the disaster worse. On January 11, 1994 - months before mass killings began - General Roméo Dallaire, who led UN peace forces, transmitted a secret message to New York. This note detailed plans for wiping out Tutsis, asking permission to seize hidden weapons stockpiles. Rather than act, top UN leaders told him to hand over the report to Rwanda’s authorities - the very regime plotting violence. Washington also blocked American staff from naming events as "genocide," sidestepping duties set by international treaty. Years afterward, President Clinton labeled that silence the deepest regret of his time in office.
Midway through July 1994, RPF leader Paul Kagame brought the slaughter to a halt by capturing Kigali through military force. Though the killing stopped, turmoil spilled across borders just as destructively. Eastern Zaire absorbed around two million fleeing Hutu, some deeply involved in mass killings; among them, the Interahamwe regrouped under a new name - the FDLR - igniting what would become the First Congo War (1996–97). That spark fed decades of chaos in eastern Congo, where conflict later claimed about six million lives. Present-day Rwanda, still led by Kagame, reports robust expansion in its economy. Between 2005 and 2012, local gacaca tribunals reviewed nearly 1.9 million cases tied to the genocide. Yet elections in 2017 and again in 2024 credited him victory with figures exceeding 99 percent - a result shadowed by the near-total absence of real rivals.[5]
Nigeria Civil War North West Africa
Oil found beneath one ethnic region intensified tensions rooted in colonial border decisions. Under British rule, distinct societies - the Muslim-majority north, Yoruba southwest, and Igbo southeast - were joined without historical ties. When independence arrived in 1960, power shifted to whoever controlled the national treasury. Discovery of oil in 1958 transformed economic stakes; revenues flowed from the Niger Delta. Over time, Nigeria emerged as the continent’s top oil exporter. Whichever group dominated the central government gained access to vast financial resources. Control of the capital meant influence over income distribution nationwide
In early 1966, a takeover mainly driven by Igbo officers ended in the killing of key political figures from the north. By July that year, a retaliatory uprising reinstated northern control - sparking waves of violence against Igbos throughout northern regions; around thirty thousand lost their lives in what came after. Because of this, Odumegwu Ojukwu, an Igbo leader serving as regional head, believed coexistence under northern rule could no longer guarantee safety or fairness for his people. Come late May 1967, he announced Biafra’s breakaway from Nigeria. Most of the country's oil reserves sat beneath lands now claimed by the fledgling republic - a fact central to Lagos resisting separation at all costs, especially under President Yakubu Gowon.
A naval blockade shaped the government's reaction. Through hunger, more lives were lost than through gunfire - guesses suggest between one and three million deaths; during peak periods, fatalities reached 3,000 to 5,000 each day. Images began spreading worldwide - swollen stomachs on starving children, the hollow cheeks of those suffering protein deficiency - turning into television’s initial large-scale emergency coverage, which challenged global agencies’ duties toward non-combatants in conflict zones. Among aid workers present, French medics such as Bernard Kouchner refused quiet cooperation demanded by the Red Cross for entry rights, choosing instead to launch Médecins Sans Frontières in 1971, aiming to voice abuses openly.[7]
January 15, 1970 marked the war's conclusion. Though Federal commander Gowon declared there would be neither winner nor loser, property taken from Igbos throughout the conflict remained unrecovered; their financial holdings likewise stayed blocked. Underlying issues - such as how oil wealth was shared and whether different groups could fairly access national funds - went unaddressed. Present-day Nigeria still contends with unrest springing from these unresolved matters. One example is the Boko Haram uprising, responsible for around 10,000 deaths in 2014 alone, including the abduction of 276 students from Chibok that April. Another stems from IPOB, a group demanding separation, labeled terrorists by authorities in 2017.
Caribbean Cuba Revolution
Backed by Washington, Batista ran Cuba like a personal estate - until he did not. Though the nation ranked high in regional income averages, its economy leaned almost entirely on sugar exports. Most profits vanished into luxury hotels run for foreign gamblers, plus land owned by a narrow circle of families. Eight out of ten export dollars came from sugar alone; roughly half sold directly to the United States. Rural hardship stayed extreme despite city glitz. By mid-decade, dissent grew too loud to ignore after constitutional rule ended in 1952. Power shifted to decrees signed behind closed doors. Distrust built slowly at first, later spreading fast - even within groups once loyal. A small band of armed rebels climbed down from mountain camps having lost little so far. Their victory seemed unlikely until suddenly it wasn’t.
Right away, Castro’s initial effort fell apart. Early on the morning of July 26, 1953, around 150 rebels struck the Moncada military post in Santiago de Cuba, aiming to ignite public revolt - yet resistance crumbled just hours later. Authorities seized Castro, put him through trial, then locked him up. After gaining freedom during a broad pardon in 1955, he moved to Mexico, where he crossed paths with Ernesto Che Guevara, an Argentinian physician, eventually forming the 26th of July Movement - the name drawn from that earlier assault. Come November 1956, Castro and Che arrived back in Cuba alongside about 80 comrades; government troops under Batista nearly wiped them out, leaving only twelve alive in mere days. Twelve individuals headed into the Sierra Maestra range, living among peasants while forming an armed resistance over twenty-four months. On New Year’s Day of 1959, Batista left Cuban soil behind.[8]
Twice, Washington attempted to undo the upheaval. Early one morning in April 1961, about 1,400 Cubans trained by American agents touched shore at a coastal inlet; without promised aerial support from President Kennedy, they were wiped out or taken prisoner before three days passed. Months later, during autumn of 1962, spy planes spotted long-range weapons hidden along northern Cuba; tension built steadily as fear spread - thirteen tense days when atomic conflict seemed possible, perhaps likely, until both superpower leaders stepped back: Moscow removed its arms if America vowed never again to attack the island. Still, Havana endured beyond that compromise. Even after the USSR fell apart, the trade ban kept tightening year after year. Meanwhile, revolutionary efforts moved outward - by late 1975 nearly twenty thousand Cuban soldiers had arrived in southern Africa supporting local fighters there, while earlier, in Bolivia’s highlands, Che Guevara met his end, shot down in 1967 with help from U.S. intelligence while attempting to spark another uprising like the one he helped win years before.
Chile South America foreign military presence
Not until Chile voted in 1970 did Washington’s limits on foreign electoral outcomes become clear. Winning by just a slim margin, Salvador Allende took office - making him the world's initial Marxist leader chosen through ballots rather than revolt. Behind the scenes, efforts brewed against him; the CIA joined forces with American businesses such as ITT and Anaconda Copper, channeling around $2 million toward rival candidates. Had those funds moved inside U.S. politics, they would have broken national laws.[10]
Swift action marked Allende's early rule. By 1971, his administration had taken control of the copper sector - seizing assets from Anaconda and Kennecott, firms behind roughly three-quarters of Chile’s output - as well as International Telephone & Telegraph. Reacting sharply, officials under President Nixon directed the CIA to intensify economic pressure. From 1971 to 1973, about $8 million flowed through covert channels: part supported strikes by transport workers that crippled exports, another portion boosted rival news outlets, while some contact unfolded between agency operatives and members of the Chilean armed forces plotting regime change. Years later, a congressional inquiry - the 1975 Church Committee - laid bare the full scope and intent of these hidden efforts.[11]
September 11, 1973 brought jet fighters roaring over Santiago as they struck the presidential palace, La Moneda; amid the chaos, Allende perished - though accounts differ sharply on whether he took his own life or was executed. Power shifted instantly to General Pinochet, who stepped into control before day's end. Brutality followed with chilling consistency: official records later revealed by Chile’s Rettig Commission counted no fewer than 3,200 people slain or vanished from 1973 through 1990. Separately, the Valech Commission confirmed nearly 28,000 instances of state-inflicted torture. Around two hundred thousand citizens fled abroad, driven out by fear and violence. Across South America, repressive regimes found coordination under Operation Condor - an intelligence alliance backed by the United States, connecting Chile, Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, Bolivia, and Brazil, officially launched in Santiago in late 1975. This web enabled cross-border abductions, secret transfers, and coordinated silencing of dissenters. One attack stood out starkly - a car explosion in Washington, D.C., on September 21, 1976 - which ended the lives of former Chilean diplomat Orlando Letelier and his U.S. coworker Ronni Moffitt.[12]
Pinochet’s regime turned into a testing site for free-market economics. Under Milton Friedman and Arnold Harberger, graduates from the University of Chicago - known as the “Chicago Boys” - moved fast to privatize industries, cut trade barriers, because democracy would have slowed them down. Growth rose at first, yet debt ballooned by the 1980s; worse still, their blueprint spread widely when the IMF enforced similar policies in Bolivia, then Argentina, later Peru during debt crises. Arrested in London during October 1998 over a Spanish request tied to violence against Spaniards, his detention set a lasting global standard: courts can prosecute human rights abuses regardless of where they happened

Russia and the United States in Modern Imperial Dynamics
Major powers often shape weaker nations through military pressure, financial leverage, and indirect governance after colonial times - no outright takeover required. Control now comes through local allies, sudden regime changes, targeted invasions, or debt ties rather than old-style rule - but power imbalances remain unchanged. Take Russia and the U.S.: each has pushed influence since the Cold War ended. Each also reveals a core flaw: dominance sparks pushback they cannot fully contain due to missing long-term authority on the ground. Resistance grows where oversight fades.
The biggest land conflict in Europe since WWII began on February 24, 2022, when Russia launched a full assault on Ukraine - its clearest neo-imperial move after 1945. Years before, pressures mounted step by step: Crimea became part of Russia in 2014 while support flowed to rebel groups in eastern Ukraine’s Donbas, efforts intensifying once Kyiv kept leaning toward the European Union. Suffering followed fast. Evidence of brutal acts near Kyiv, especially in Bucha, came well ahead of an ICC order in March 2023 charging President Putin with forcibly transferring Ukrainian minors abroad. Before 2022 ended, more than six point seven million Ukrainians fled their homes, tracked across Europe by UNHCR. At the same time, Moscow halted gas deliveries, sparking power shortages that pushed Western nations to speed up energy projects reducing ties to Russian fuel sources.[15]
From 2015 onward, the Wagner Group - linked to Yevgeny Prigozhin - carried Russian reach into Syria, then later into Libya by 2019. Moving further, deployments began in Mali and the Central African Republic starting in 2021, followed by Sudan in 2023, allowing Moscow to exert power without official troop deployment. In Africa's heartland, protection promises were swapped for access to small-scale gold mines: force turned quietly into profit. Yet cracks appeared suddenly when, in June 2023, Prigozhin mobilized tanks aimed at the capital before halting short. Then came silence after a plane fell from the sky that August - the event underlining how unstable such shadow ventures truly are.
A familiar sequence repeats in how the U.S. has acted in Iraq and Afghanistan. Early 2003 saw American forces enter Iraq, justified by assertions of hidden chemical arms - claims later shown to be baseless. Months prior to international debate, a leaked UK government note from summer 2002 revealed awareness that evidence was shaped ahead of decisions, not after. Images emerged in spring 2004 from Abu Ghraib prison, exposing organized abuse of captives by American troops; while lower-ranking service members were punished, top leaders escaped legal scrutiny - an imbalance weakening trust among Middle Eastern populations. After state structures collapsed post-invasion, an opening formed for extremist groups; one such group, ISIS, took Mosul in early June 2014 using just fifteen hundred men to scatter thirty thousand Iraqi soldiers, despite years of extensive training and funding provided by Washington.[16]
Twenty years of military involvement in Afghanistan totaled around $2.3 trillion, according to Brown University's Watson Institute in 2021. Though intended to establish lasting stability, the effort saw the locally trained armed forces disintegrate rapidly after American withdrawal. On August 15, 2021, control of Kabul shifted back to the Taliban - exactly the faction displaced by U.S. actions in 2001. Shortly afterward, female access to high school was prohibited, undoing years of schooling initiatives. Despite sustained international engagement, governance did not settle into a durable system. Instead, authority consolidated under the revived Taliban regime without meaningful internal opposition.
South Africa and Brazil Democracy
What counts as democracy goes beyond casting ballots. Power must be spread through working structures, those in charge kept in check, while economic discontent - sparked by leadership shifts that leave wealth untouched - is addressed. After escaping long stretches of autocratic control, South Africa and Brazil introduced open voting systems. For a stretch, their frameworks operated as intended. Yet each showed how hollow democratic appearances become when deep inequality stays unchallenged.
One morning in February 1990, Nelson Mandela stepped out of prison following nearly three decades behind bars. That moment set into motion what would become an exceptional shift toward democracy four years later. When April 27, 1994 arrived, around 20 million people cast ballots - many for the very first time. Victory went to the African National Congress, capturing just over 62 percent of support. A new chapter unfolded when, one year on, Archbishop Desmond Tutu led the launch of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. More than seven thousand stories were told before it concluded its work. Those willing to confess their role in political violence could receive amnesty - a path others later borrowed. Courts in Rwanda and Sierra Leone adapted similar ideas in their own efforts at justice
Yet economic divides barely shifted during the shift to democracy. Instead of spreading ownership, the ANC backed private markets, selling off government firms while leaving mineral riches concentrated in old hands, cementing inequalities within new laws. Into this gap stepped Jacob Zuma, whose years in office saw steady dismantling of what public systems survived. A judicial inquiry led by Chief Justice Raymond Zondo began work in 2018, uncovering deep corporate meddling - Gupta allies and cronies siphoning funds through Eskom, Transnet, and the national airline, draining some R500 billion, near $27 billion, from state coffers. Come 2023, joblessness hit 32.9 percent; blackouts lasting up to twelve hours each day choked production; then, in May 2024, voters gave the ANC just 40.2 percent - its weakest result since taking power, breaking its solo rule and requiring shared governance.
Brazil's democracy moved along its own path. Winning in 2002, Luiz Inácio "Lula" da Silva became the nation’s first leader from a working-class background; his Bolsa Família initiative helped lift around 20 million out of deep poverty by 2010. Yet those advances depended heavily on income from commodities, lacking deeper changes to systems. By contrast, when Jair Bolsonaro took office in 2018, forest loss in the Amazon climbed sharply - reaching 11,568 square kilometers by 2022, the largest yearly total since 2006 according to INPE. Meanwhile, delays and missteps during the pandemic vaccination effort were tied by public health officials to an estimated surge in fatalities numbering in the hundreds of thousands
Lula took office following a narrow victory - 50.9% to 49.1% - in the 2022 runoff, marking Brazil’s most divided vote in recent memory. Despite the result, Bolsonaro did not acknowledge defeat. In Brasília, on January 8, 2023, crowds loyal to him forced their way into the presidential palace, Congress, and supreme court buildings - an echo of Washington’s unrest two years earlier. He himself was abroad, staying in Florida while authorities detained over fifteen hundred individuals linked to the breach; convictions followed for more than two hundred. Around the same time, late in 2023, Argentina shifted course dramatically by electing Javier Milei, an outspoken critic of mainstream politics whose campaign imagery included wielding a chainsaw, fueled by long-standing struggles with rising prices and dealings with international lenders. Together, these events - from Pretoria to São Paulo - point toward something deeper: persistent inequality may leave democratic structures standing but erodes them quietly inside, as politicians turn public hardship into challenges against voting integrity directly
