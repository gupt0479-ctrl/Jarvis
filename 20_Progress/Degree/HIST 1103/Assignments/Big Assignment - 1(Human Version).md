# Human
Three Nations Shifted From 1950
China (East Asia)

Beginning in 1950, Mao’s China remained weak - its economy broken, rural areas poor, lacking aid from Western nations. Though Soviet assistance came at first, it vanished once disagreements grew stronger by the early 1960s. Then came the Great Leap Forward (1958 - 62), which fell apart, causing history’s worst famine - between 15 and 45 million lives lost (Jones 2025; Spence 2013, 553). After that disaster, the Cultural Revolution unfolded (1966 - 76): schools shut down for ten years, violence took perhaps two million people (Spence 2013, 575). By contrast, when Deng Xiaoping shifted direction in 1978, changes began - market-style policies, special zones near coasts, steady yearly growth close to 10%, continuing thirty years, pulling around 800 million from extreme need (Jones 2025). Following 2012, under Xi Jinping’s leadership, China took firmer positions - expanding claims in the South China Sea, tightening control in Hong Kong, while advancing worldwide influence via massive construction projects linked to the Belt and Road initiative.

Iran (Middle East)

Back in 1951, Mossadegh took control of the oil industry; by 1953, though, American and British intelligence had orchestrated a covert move - Operation Ajax - that ousted him, reinstating the Shah without limits on his authority (Keddie 2006, 134). For twenty years after, state terror under SAVAK kept tightening, fueling discontent until Khomeini led a revolt in 1979, swapping out monarchy for an Islamic Republic (Jones 2025; Keddie 2006, 230). Then came Iraq’s invasion in September 1980 - an eight-year conflict ending near one million lives lost. Following 1989, shifts in power moved Iran back and forth between reformists and conservatives, while economic penalties tied to nuclear ambitions worsened internal struggles. Then came 2022: a turning point arrived when Mahsa Amini died under arrest by the morality police, sparking unrest unmatched in scale since the revolution began (Jones 2025).

Vietnam (Southeast Asia)

Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh defeated France at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954; soon afterward, the Geneva Accords split Vietnam along the 17th parallel (Karnow 1997, 197). Though American military presence grew past half a million soldiers, stability never took root - South Vietnam collapsed when Saigon was lost in April 1975. Following reunification, Hanoi introduced socialist economic policies, moved into Cambodia by force in 1978, which then triggered China’s retaliatory invasion one year later. Shift began in 1986 with Doi Moi: centralized control of markets faded while individual businesses gained space, sparking sustained expansion above seven percent each year throughout the next decade. Normalization with Washington happened in 1995, meanwhile global trade integration advanced upon joining the WTO in 2007 (Jones 2025; Karnow 1997, 44).

Key People and Events

China

Mao Zedong: Beginning his rule in 1949, Mao Zedong remained leader until 1976. Intervention in Korea during 1950 helped secure territorial boundaries. By 1964, a nuclear test signaled technological reach. Diplomatic contact emerged through meetings with Nixon in 1972. Yet under his direction came the deadliest episodes in China’s past. The Party's official verdict - "70% correct, 30% wrong", understates the damage considerably (Spence 2013, 544).

The Cultural Revolution (1966 - 1976): Years passed without university classes. Ten full ones, while thinkers and leaders vanished into fields or darker places. As many as two million people died amid shifting waves of accusation and punishment. Twice removed from power, Deng Xiaoping spent time assembling tractors in a factory town (Spence 2013, 572). What stayed behind was not policy but an unease: chaos might return if thought goes unchecked.

Deng Xiaoping's 1978 Reforms: Out of the late 1970s emerged a different China, dismantled commune farming, built Special Economic Zones, introduced market incentives without loosening party rule. Instead of ideology guiding choices, success became the measure. His line - "doesn't matter if a cat is black or white as long as it catches mice" (Jones 2025), captured the shift from doctrine to results. Factories rose within new Special Economic Zones where global investment flowed freely. Growth followed swiftly, lifting millions from poverty faster than history had recorded elsewhere. Yet, limits remained clear when events at Tiananmen revealed boundaries beyond which change would not be tolerated.

Iran

Mohammad Mossadegh: Iran's elected prime minister, 1951 - 1953. He stood as living proof democracy might work in the country. Yet foreign interference cut it short. A covert mission called Operation Ajax pulled him down. That moment left behind deep bitterness. Anger shaped political currents across seven decades. Threads from his downfall clearly stretch into the upheaval of 1979 (Keddie 2006, 135).

The 1979 Islamic Revolution: Surprisingly, proved a mass movement could topple a U.S. - backed military state. Power shifted dramatically when Khomeini introduced *velayat-e faqih*, assigning religious leaders official political control - a concept previously unseen across Islamic governance structures. As a result, ties between Iran and the United States broke apart abruptly. Neighboring royal rule systems felt immediate unease. Conflict followed soon after, dragging into a full-scale invasion that lasted nearly a decade (Jones 2025; Keddie 2006, 238).

The Iran-Iraq War (1980 - 1988): A sudden move by Saddam shaped what came next - he thought Iran, fresh from revolution, would fold fast. Instead, conflict dragged on, filling years with poison gas, scorched cities, and drained treasuries - no border shifted when it finally ended. Tehran stayed standing; its leaders grew tougher, more convinced they could outlast any threat. That belief helped push Iraq toward Kuwait in 1990 (Keddie 2006, 252).

Vietnam

Ho Chi Minh: Outliving two Western empires, he anchored his cause in village allegiance instead of military might. His passing came in September 1969 - years before unity arrived. Not until six years later did the nation unite. A city now bears his name, marking how deeply he lives on in Vietnam’s collective awareness (Karnow 1997, 148).

The Tet Offensive (January 1968): Over 100 cities hit simultaneously during the New Year ceasefire. Though communist units suffered some 45,000 casualties within weeks - marking a clear defeat on the battlefield, the impact elsewhere shifted dramatically. Footage showing flames near the U.S. Embassy spread fast into homes across America. Walter Cronkite called the war a stalemate, and President Johnson dropped out of the race (Karnow 1997, 537).

Doi Moi (1986): Collective farms faded out - private businesses emerged alongside access for overseas investors, though the ruling party kept authority. As a result of these shifts, rice production rose so much that by ten years later Vietnam ranked among leading exporters worldwide. Growth in GDP stayed above 7% each year during the 1990s; at the same time, fewer people lived in poverty (Jones 2025).

Deep Dive Into Part C

Deng Xiaoping’s Changes and How They Shaped China

China’s economy stood at about $149 billion in 1978, drawing little interest from overseas investors. Under Deng’s reforms, families took over farming duties once handled by collectives - and crop yields climbed sharply, rising from 305 million tons to 407 million tons between 1978 and 1984 (Spence 2013, 617). Soon after, zones like Shenzhen opened for targeted development; what began as a small coastal town of 30,000 swelled past 10 million residents by 2000, exporting more each year than the entire nation did before reform (Jones 2025). Accession to the World Trade Organization in late 2001 cemented its role within international trade networks. Eighty years ago, few could have predicted how fast growth would reshape lives - by 2019, output topped $14 trillion. Nearly 800 million moved beyond survival-level hardship during that span, a shift unmatched before. Commodity demand from China lifted exporting economies across Africa and Latin America; cheap Chinese manufacturing held down consumer prices in wealthy countries while simultaneously displacing factory workers in the U.S. and Europe, feeding political discontent through the 2010s.

On June 4, 1989, a boundary took shape - sharp, unyielding. Through the weeks before, crowds of students and city laborers gathered in Tiananmen Square, voices raised for change. Then came orders from Deng; troops moved in, lives ended - numbers uncertain but high. The Tank Man photograph became one of the most reproduced images of the 20th century and told the world exactly what Deng's model was: prosperity without political rights (Spence 2013, 630). Ever after, that choice echoes in disputes over markets, data flows, islands, cities - not loud each time, yet always present.

The 1979 Iranian Islamic Revolution and Long Term Changes

Early in 1953, events unfolded that would shape Iran’s future. The removal of Mossadegh through Operation Ajax revealed how easily national autonomy could be set aside when foreign oil concerns came into play (Keddie 2006, 134). For more than twenty years, SAVAK ensured people remembered this reality. Come 1977, resistance to the Shah brought together an unlikely mix - religious leaders, socialists, business owners, patriots - all bound less by common goals than by what they stood against. On February 1, 1979, Khomeini arrived back amid a sea of people stretching beyond sight. Top-ranking religious scholars gained ultimate authority under his concept of *velayat-e faqih*, sidelining democratic bodies - an arrangement unseen before across Islamic nations. The U.S. Embassy was seized on November 4, 1979; 52 diplomats held for 444 days, breaking U.S.-Iran relations and helping end Jimmy Carter's presidency (Jones 2025). Without warning, Saddam launched an offensive on September 22, 1980. Chemical arms appeared repeatedly during combat; one single attack in Halabja that March took about 5,000 Kurdish civilians at Halabja in March 1988 alone. Iran lost between 300,000 and 500,000 military dead but came out intact - survival became the regime's founding narrative (Keddie 2006, 253).

Across regions, Tehran expanded power using allied factions - Hezbollah emerged in Lebanon by 1982, followed years later by ties to Houthi elements in Yemen, then Iraqi Shia units after 2003, along with militant circles in Gaza, achieving presence distant borders would otherwise limit (Jones 2025). Within its own boundaries, protests like the 2009 Green campaign and the 2022 unrest - involving 160 urban centers, more than 15,000 detained, over 500 dead - reveal a state formed through mass revolt now quelling public dissent much as the monarchy once did.

The Tet Offensive changed how people saw the war

Come winter 1967, Westmoreland assured Congress that victory in Vietnam seemed near. Then came January 30–31, 1968: nearly 80,000 communist troops struck more than 100 cities mid-Tet truce, even breaching the U.S. Embassy grounds in Saigon. Fighting raged for 26 days in Hue, where mass graves later revealed about 2,800 slain non-combatants (Karnow 1997, 545). Though the attack failed on battlefield terms - with some 45,000 insurgents killed early and Viet Cong units shattered - the political impact shifted everything. What followed had no impact on politics. A photo by Eddie Adams - showing Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan executing a handcuffed man in Saigon on February 1 - earned a Pulitzer and spread fast worldwide. By February 27, Cronkite declared on CBS news that victory seemed unreachable. It is said Johnson reacted sharply: if Cronkite turned away, so would average U.S. citizens - a quiet sign his government’s trustworthiness had unraveled completely (Karnow 1997, 548).

That Tuesday in March, McCarthy took 42 percent of the vote in New Hampshire. By month’s end, Johnson exited the race. The Pentagon turned down Westmoreland’s push for more than two hundred thousand soldiers. A year later, Nixon carried the fall election pledging an unclear path to peace. Gradually, American troops pulled back under Vietnamization, inching toward the accord signed in Paris come early ’73 (Jones 2025). What stuck longest wasn’t just the event, but the lasting doubt it planted - official accounts of conflict often diverge from what unfolds on the ground. This skepticism influenced how journalists were allowed near combat zones during the 1991 Gulf War, while reappearing plainly when evidence behind the 2003 Iraq invasion came under scrutiny (Karnow 1997, 19).
