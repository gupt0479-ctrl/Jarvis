Argentina has suffered from chronic inflation and macroeconomic instability. The government has spent more money than it has collected in taxes consistently over the years. The government has run on a fiscal deficit, in 57 of the past 65 years. This has caused the government to regularly print more money or borrow a lot from international lenders.  The country’s small domestic financial markets prevent the country from funding its sovereign debt internally.  With the country lacking domestic credit or internation market access, the government has turned to the central bank to print more money. This has led to nine debt defaults, with the most notable being in 2001 with a $100 billion default. The domestic currency has repeatedly lost its value, wiping out local savings and driving a preference for US money. The inflation has become structural and peaked during the hyperinflation crises of 1989 and 1990, where the annual price increases exceeded 3,000%. This devastated the economy and forced early presidential transitions. This has caused an erosion of wealth and investment. The constant shifts in the economic rules have destroyed investor confidence. It has become standard practice for citizens to move their money abroad to protect it. The high inflation has crippled long-term planning, lowered productivity, and has dragged large portions of the population into poverty.

Argentina has suffered from extreme political instability and institutional polarization. The country has lacked continuous democratic governance or a stable political consensus.  The military has repeatedly overthrown civilian governments, creating a cycle of political disruption.  The 1976 coup was led by Jorge Rafael Videla that overthrew President Isabel Peron and started the “Dirty War”.  This coup established a regime that dismantled democratic institutions. The regime censored the press and banned trade unions. Under the guise of eliminating leftist subversion and guerrilla groups, the state imprisoned political activists and members of the groups. The state used clandestine detention centers, torture, and tossed drugged prisoners into the ocean. They also kidnaped the babies born to imprisoned political activists and shortly executed them. “Ordering the disappearance of thousands of suspected dissidents in Argentina’s so called “Dirty War”. Military officials took some of the “disappeared” in helicopters above the ocean and pushed them to their deaths below.” (Jones, pg 269). The war ended in 1983 when the military dictatorship collapsed following its defeat in the Falklands War and an economic crisis.  After the loss of the Falklands War the military’s credibility was shattered. There were massive protests across Argentina led by civil rights groups like the Madres de Plaza de Mayo and the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo, who demanded to know about the thousands of citizens who had “disappeared”. The military was forced to call for general elections. Raul Alfonsin was elected to power on October 30, 1983, and ended the period of state terrorism and restored constitutional rule. 

How do you think the multiple coups have affected Argentina?
