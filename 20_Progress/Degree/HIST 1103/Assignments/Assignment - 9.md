**Kenya and Rwanda - Assignment 9**

Kenya:

Similar to many other countries we have been discussing in this class, Kenya had similar issues relating to colonization. The British controlled large parts of Kenya towards the end of the 19th century, “as part of the British East Africa Protectorate, setting Kenya’s borders following World War I in 1920 when they claimed it as a colony.” (Jones, p.221) Under British rule, British settlers took over some of the fertile highlands that had previously been claimed territory by the Kikuyu, the majority ethnic group of Kenya. European settlers introduced cash crops like tea, coffee, sugarcane, and cotton to the soils, growing these for profit to the central government they were designated under. 

As time passed, and World War II cleared, Kenya wanted its independence from English control, this leads us to our most important event in recent history of Kenya, the Mau Mau Rebellion. Beginning in 1952, the Kikuyu took up arms against the British civilians and other Kikuyu that had been collaborating with Europe. On October 20th, the British governor declared a state of emergency in Kenya, due to the mass execution of its citizens residing there. British police swarmed the area and began capturing Kikuyu members left and right. Despite this, some managed to flee and build up military strength to fight again against the British. This cat and mouse game lasted for roughly 4 years, and it came up unsuccessful. The British had killed close to 20,000 Kikuyu members. Then, both during, and following the event, imprisoned another 90,000 forcing them to live in extremely poor conditions. It was an extremely unsuccessful operation by casualty count. However, due to the fear of another uprising, the British government decided to station 50,000 troops within Kenya to maintain safety of its citizens, which ended up being extremely costly, despite other world events that made Britain recall its troops. Once they had called for a return to their core nation, Kenya had essentially gained its independence shortly after, with a key figure in Jomo Kenyatta being put under control, a key figure in the Mau Mau uprising.

Rwanda:

In Rwanda, two key ethnic groups took up the core of the country, the Hutus and Tutsis. Though, they can arguably be described as social groups instead of different ethnic players due to their lack of key differences besides job roles. Once Europeans entered the picture however, that relatedness went out the window. One key tactic by Europeans when colonizing would be to create uproar between key groups of the preexisting nation, in turn creating a social void where another group can step in and claim power to designate new borders. During World War I, Belgians took over Germany’s claim of Rwanda, and started to expand upon the newly claimed racial divide between Tutsis and Hutus. In 1934, Belgium lent some power to the “elite” Tutsis, giving them some social power over that of their counterpart. However, as we discussed earlier, there wasn’t any real way to tell the difference between the two ethnic groups, leading to the assignment of identity cards. Every single citizen was given an identity card that as part of it, would have a tell to their ethnicity. The strangest part, the key tell that Belgian scientists used to tell the difference between the two groups… was a matter of millimeters in nose size.

As ethnic division took more shape, and the divide became more prominent, something unexpected happened. After years of Tutsis being on top, Belgians bent to the other side and empowered the Hutus to fight back against the Tutsis. Following years of being pushed around by their once friendly comrades, Hutus realized that they had a numbers advantage, all they had needed was unison to take down the Tutsis. After all, Tutsis only held roughly a 1:4 ratio of the population when put in comparison to the Hutus. As the outbreak began, Tutsis quickly shed thousands of civilians to the brutal attacks. Hutus quickly took government power, putting a dictator in place who coined the Tutsis as “cockroaches.” Following many more attacks, most of the Tutsis either fled the country, or had died. 

To reach a climax, the biggest event of this country was it’s genocide. In the 90’s, a group of Tutsis raided the country under the name “Rwandese Patriotic Front.” On April 6th, 1994, a missile struck down a Hutu plane that was carrying many high level officials, no evidence of who shot it has been discovered to this day. Because of this event however, Hutu nationalists blamed it on the Tutsis, and the genocide began. Instead of a new leader taking over following the death of the president, the next leader in line was killed by Hutu extremists, because: “Uwilingiyimana was moderate and would not support their planned genocide, so she had to be eliminated.” Inevitably, extremists took over, and the killing began. Unfortunately, over the next 100 days of history, close to 1 million civilians, including Tutsis and moderate Hutus that were too “soft” on their counterpart were lost, making it amongst the top 5 genocides in history considering total death count.

## My Response

**Somalia**

Somalia sits at the tip of the Horn of Africa, population roughly 19.4 million as of 2025. Unlike most African states, it's ethnically homogeneous — 85% Somali, 98% Sunni Muslim. Its divisions are clan-based: six major clan-families each subdivided into competing sub-clans fighting for political power.

Muhammad Siad Barre is the single biggest driver of Somalia's long-term discontent. He seized power in a military coup in October 1969, suspended the constitution, banned political parties, and installed the Supreme Revolutionary Council. For the first decade he preached against tribalism. After losing the 1977–78 Ogaden War to Ethiopia, he used tribalism as his governing tool.

By 1986, Barre had deployed the Red Berets — recruited from his own Marehan sub-clan — against rival clans. By end of 1988, they had killed an estimated 5,000 Isaaq, poisoned water wells, and burned grazing grounds. Amnesty International named his regime among the world's most repressive that same year.

When the United Somali Congress ousted Barre in January 1991, no functional state remained. Clan militias filled the vacuum. By 1992, more than 50,000 Somalis had died in fighting and 300,000 more from starvation. The UN intervened. Eighteen US Marines died in Mogadishu in October 1993. Clinton withdrew.

As Jones writes, "The legacy of clan-based conflict, colonialism, and the Cold War have left Somalia a very divided and torn nation — a failed state — despite the fact that it began over sixty years ago as an independent country with a potentially promising future" (Jones, p. 232). Life expectancy in 2020 was 56.5 years. Al-Shabaab killed 350 people in a single 2017 truck bombing in Mogadishu. The vacuum Barre created has never been filled.

**Democratic Republic of Congo**

The DRC is the largest country in sub-Saharan Africa — population around 100 million, sitting on an estimated $24 trillion in mineral wealth: cobalt, copper, uranium, coltan. As many as 6 million people have died in its civil wars since the mid-1990s.

The root cause is Mobutu Sese Seko. He took power in a 1960 coup and ruled for over three decades, renaming the country Zaire and himself "The all-powerful warrior who, because of his endurance and inflexible will to win, will go from conquest to conquest, leaving fire in his wake." The US backed him as an anti-communist ally. By 1989, Zaire had defaulted on its international debt largely due to government embezzlement. When rebel forces ousted him in mid-1997, Mobutu fled to Morocco as one of the world's wealthiest men, leaving behind no functional army, administration, or institutions.

The civil war that followed drew in troops from seven neighboring countries. Laurent Kabila, who replaced Mobutu, was assassinated in 2001. Fighting in the eastern Kivu region has never fully stopped. As of 2024, roughly 100 non-state armed militias operate in the country, and 358,000 Congolese were displaced that year alone.

Three decades of Mobutu left the state with nothing to govern with. Every crisis since is a direct consequence.

What do you think matters more for long-term stability: removing a bad leader, or building institutions capable of surviving after he's gone?
