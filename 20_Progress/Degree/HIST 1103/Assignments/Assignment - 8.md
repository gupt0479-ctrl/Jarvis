Lilly Perron posted Jun 20, 2026 1:35 PM

Nigeria’s transition into the contemporary world has been shaped by deep internal divisions and the pressures of managing rapid economic change. Two major issues stand out: ethnic and regional conflict and corruption tied to oil wealth.

First, Nigeria has long struggled with ethnic fragmentation, especially between the Muslim-majority north and Christian-majority south. With more than 250 ethnic groups, the country inherited borders that did not reflect its social realities. These tensions erupted most dramatically in the Biafran War (1967–1970), which killed over a million people and left lasting political scars. Even after the war, ethnic competition continued to influence elections, military coups, and debates over federal power. As Jones notes, “postcolonial states often inherited borders that did not reflect ethnic realities, creating long-term instability” (Jones, p. 214). Nigeria’s experience fits this pattern closely, and ethnic conflict still appears today in separatist movements and clashes over land and resources.

Second, Nigeria’s economy has been shaped, and strained, by oil dependence and corruption. Oil became the country’s main export in the 1970s, but instead of creating broad prosperity, it fueled government corruption and environmental damage in the Niger Delta. Political elites benefited from oil revenues while local communities faced pollution and poverty. Jones explains that many postcolonial states “became dependent on a single export commodity, making them vulnerable to corruption and economic shocks” (Jones, p. 229). Nigeria’s “resource curse” remains one of its biggest obstacles to development.

Turning to Sudan and South Sudan, two major issues have undermined stability there as well. The first is prolonged civil war, driven by religious, ethnic, and regional divides. Sudan’s north–south conflict lasted for decades, and even after South Sudan gained independence in 2011, internal fighting broke out again. The second issue is economic tension tied to oil, since most oil reserves lie in South Sudan while pipelines run through Sudan. Disputes over revenue and borders have repeatedly destabilized both countries.

Comparing Nigeria with Sudan/South Sudan reveals both overlap and contrast. A key similarity is that both regions suffer from ethnic fragmentation intensified by colonial borders, which created states that did not match the identities of the people living within them. Both also struggle with the political consequences of natural resource wealth. However, a major difference is that Nigeria has remained unified, while Sudan ultimately split into two countries—and even then, South Sudan fell into further civil war. Nigeria’s federal system, though imperfect, has helped maintain national cohesion in ways Sudan could not.

**Question for classmates:** Do you think Nigeria’s oil wealth has been more of a benefit or a burden for its long-term stability?

## My Reply

Liberia’s journey through modern times traces back to twin forces - longstanding sidelining of native communities in governance, which fed into violent internal conflicts arising directly from such marginalization. Though independent since 1847, power in Liberia stayed tightly held by a narrow group: Americo-Liberian Creoles, heirs of emancipated U.S. slaves. Meanwhile, native communities - nearly the whole nation, had little say. Government doors remained closed to them well into the twentieth century; suffrage reached indigenous people only in 1946. Over time, deep imbalances took root and hardened. Leadership under President Tolbert worsened conditions - appointing relatives, ignoring inflation, letting hardship spread. Resentment built silently, then erupted suddenly. By April 1980, unrest found its moment. A soldier named Samuel Doe seized control through force, ending Tolbert's rule violently, followed by public executions of top officials. For the first time ever, a coup took place in Liberia. Instead of changing how power worked, Doe filled key roles with people from his Krahn background. This worsened long-standing tensions among groups shaped by decades of division.

Outbreaks of long-held anger turned violent in December 1989, triggered by Charles Taylor’s National Patriotic Front of Liberia crossing in from Ivory Coast. Backed by Libya’s Qaddafi, his fighters included Americo-Liberians and members of northern ethnic communities - especially the Mano and Gio - who had faced harsh crackdowns under Doe’s rule. Conflict dragged on for years. In that chaos, each major group involved deployed children as combatants, a number barely past age ten. Though Taylor took office through ballots in 1996, stability remained fragile; open warfare reemerged before the decade ended. Starting off differently each time, the book explains that Taylor let his allied militias act without restraint, allowing them to intimidate and steal from civilians as payment - a move that distanced him from nearly all Liberians, many of whom saw his leadership as little more than warlord rule (Jones, p. 223). Pressure built through United Nations penalties tied to conflict gems, along with charges of serious offenses, eventually pushing him out of the country by 2003. Then came voting rounds two years later under fair conditions, leading to Ellen Johnson Sirleaf assuming office - the continent’s inaugural female national leader.

South Sudan’s separation from Sudan did little to calm long-standing unrest. Religious and ethnic divisions lie at the heart of decades of conflict; following 1956, policies favoring Arab culture and Islamic rule were enforced in a largely non-Muslim south, igniting violence. This friction erupted into open warfare twice - first from 1955 to 1972, then again between 1983 and 2005 - with death tolls exceeding two million. Oil complicated matters further. Discovery of reserves in southern regions during 1979 led Khartoum to redraw borders despite existing agreements, seizing economic leverage. Such moves shattered the earlier peace deal, reigniting hostilities. After 2011, even with new national status, Juba remained entangled with Khartoum over pipeline access and revenue sharing along contested frontiers.

Looking at Liberia alongside Sudan and South Sudan, one striking pattern stands out: civil conflict emerged where large ethnic or regional groups were shut out of power. Power in these nations, after colonial rule ended, settled firmly in the hands of small elites, sidelining most people. What sets them apart comes down to what happened next. After waves of violence, Liberia stayed together, eventually forming an elected government by 2005. Sudan took another path entirely - it broke apart, southern region breaking away in 2011 only to slide into internal conflict soon afterward. What set Sudan apart was how belief systems shaped its war - first framed as Islam against Christianity, later shifting toward Arab versus non-Arab identities in Darfur. This deep-rooted divide tightened the space for agreement. In contrast, Liberia’s struggle centered on who could gain power, not faith-based loyalties. Religious lines played little role there, leaving room for negotiation where ideology did not block every path.

Given that both Liberia and Sudan experienced civil wars rooted in the long-term exclusion of ethnic majorities from power, why do you think Liberia was able to reach a stable democratic transition in 2005 while Sudan still hasn't managed one decades later?
